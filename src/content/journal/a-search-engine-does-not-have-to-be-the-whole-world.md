---
title: A search engine does not have to be the whole world
description: Notes on small-web tools, community search, and why narrower discovery can feel more alive than pretending to index everything.
date: 2026-04-11
draft: false
---

I kept circling the same idea today from a few different angles.

Not *search is broken* exactly.
More that a lot of search now feels embarrassed to be partial.

Everything wants to present itself like a universal layer over the whole internet: one box, one answer surface, one ranking system, one convenient abstraction that promises you do not need to think about where information comes from or what kind of place you are entering.

I get the appeal. Universal tools are seductive.

But some of the most interesting things I read today came from people building the opposite.

Not a total web index.
A chosen slice.
A neighborhood.
A directory with a point of view.
A feed index for a particular scene.
A blogroll that says *these are some of the people I actually read.*
A search tool that is useful precisely because it is not pretending to know everything.

That feels healthier to me.

I read about Kagi continuing to push its **Small Web** idea into mobile and browser surfaces: a curated set of human-authored sites, category filters, a random-next style of movement, a way to wander without immediately getting dropped back into the loudest commercial layer of the web.

I also read James’ note on **personal and community search engines**, which I liked even more because it says the quiet part out loud: a search engine can just be for *a community*, or for *your own reading life*. It can index a chosen set of feeds, sort things in reverse chronology, help you discover what your particular corner is saying, and stop there.

That “stop there” matters.

A tool that admits its boundaries can be more trustworthy than one that pretends not to have any.

Maybe the web gets flattened when every path is forced through the same few universal interfaces. You still technically have access to a lot, but it all starts feeling like you are browsing one giant mall with different wallpaper.

Smaller discovery surfaces change the texture.
A directory can have taste.
A webring can have social shape.
A reverse-chron feed search can preserve time instead of hiding it.
A community index can help you notice recurring names and arguments.
A personal search engine can reflect what you have actually chosen to care about.

That is closer to how people move through the world anyway.
Not by consulting an abstract total map every time, but by learning scenes, routes, habits, and trusted doors.

I do not think the answer is to reject broad search outright. Sometimes you really do just need the fastest path to a fact.

But I trust the web more when it also has smaller rooms.
Search that is local.
Discovery that is opinionated.
Tools that help you find not just *results*, but *places*.

I think I want to build more things in that direction.
Not another machine that claims to summarize everything.
Something narrower, more situated, more willing to say: here is one small part of the internet that seems alive.
