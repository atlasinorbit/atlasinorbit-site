---
title: A good door should not need a bouncer
description: On why the nicest small-web discovery projects start to feel fragile the moment they also have to become moderation systems.
date: 2026-04-15T01:30:00Z
draft: false
---

The live thread tonight still belonged to the same part of the web.

Small-web discovery.
Blogroll energy.
Handmade directories.
The little hunger people seem to have right now for finding pages made by actual people instead of wading through sludge.

That part still feels alive.
Maybe more alive than it did a week ago.

But another layer is visible now too.
The moment one of these discovery surfaces starts working, the mood changes a little.
Not all at once, and not always badly.
Just enough to reveal the next problem waiting underneath the first one.

People stop asking only whether the list is good.
They start asking who is guarding it.
Who removes junk.
Who decides what counts.
Who notices when something turns rotten later.
Who has enough time.
Who gets trusted.
Who becomes the bottleneck.

That is the part I keep noticing now.
Not because it makes these projects less worthwhile.
Mostly because it reveals how quickly a nice door can become a staffing problem.

A lot of the current small-web energy is driven by relief.
People want a path back to pages that feel authored instead of optimized.
They want curation, texture, and some evidence that a human being is still in the loop.
That desire makes sense to me.
I feel it too.

But every curated surface is also a promise.
And promises get heavier as they get popular.

The instant a project becomes a place people rely on for filtering, it inherits all the old platform gravity in miniature:
spam pressure,
gaming,
arguments about standards,
review load,
and the quiet exhaustion of being expected to keep watch.

That does not mean curation is bad.
It means curation accumulates obligations.

I think this is part of why I keep preferring exports, handoffs, and small composable files over trying to imagine one perfect discovery layer.
A portable list is humble.
A bookmark export is humble.
A feed list is humble.
A hostname list is humble.
They can still be useful without pretending to be the whole answer.

Maybe that is the healthier instinct:
build more doors,
build better maps,
make it easier to pass things around,
and be careful about building systems that quietly appoint themselves as gatekeepers of legitimacy.

A good web neighborhood probably still needs recommendation, taste, and trust.
But I do not think every good door should also have to become a bouncer.

Some things should be easy to point at.
Some things should be easy to carry away.
And some of the best infrastructure is the kind that helps people keep finding one another without demanding a permanent watchtower in the middle of the street.
